// About this review

This Detailed Modular Review assesses whether St Village Preston can be delivered as a precision modular building. It tests the town planning design against HAAVN’s modular design principles, and sets out the logistics, site, compliance and cost implications of a modular delivery.

All figures are indicative. They rest on the assumptions and caveats in section 07, which should be read with this report.

St Village Preston is a good candidate for a precision modular solution. A high proportion of the existing design can be delivered in modular, the efficient ratio of GFA to NSA is retained, and the façade already sits on a grid that suits module widths. // Build approach

Traditional base. Modular from level 1.

Traditional build elements

The initial levels will all be built traditionally, with the first modules connecting directly to the slab and building up from there. The structural elements will need to be reworked to transfer the loading from the modular elements.

Transfer slab

Because the modules weigh less than concrete, the transfer slab could potentially reduce to 60% of its size, which may benefit the project’s shadowing and setbacks.

Modular build elements

The modular build elements begin from level one. There may be an opportunity for additional modular efficiency by using bathroom pods and investigating retail modules on the edge of the lower levels. This has not been considered within the scope of this report.

  • Traditional base: basement, ground and mezzanine, and the transfer slab. Modules connect directly to the slab from level 1 and build up to level 14.
  • The cores stay traditional. Modules are lighter than concrete, so the transfer slab could reduce to about 60% of its size.
  • Possible extra efficiency from bathroom pods and retail modules on the lower levels, not in scope for this report.

// Modular design summary

A full interior recut. The envelope stays.

The architectural plans will require a full interior rework to create a suitable modular solution. There are multiple cross-level design flaws which prevent this from being a simple adjustment to the designs.

A new design will need to fit the parameters in the town planning submission. As the project has already been submitted to DFP for permit approval, any changes should not impact the building envelope.

Thankfully, the exterior design elements lend themselves perfectly to a modular solution: the façade is a repeating 3.5 m wide pattern, lining up with efficient module dimensions.

Reengineering the core to meet modular requirements
06
Designing consistent balcony floor plans between levels
07
Ensuring wet area connection to same module corridor risers
08
Repositioning the core in Building One to avoid it being exposed on the higher levels
  • A full interior recut within the lodged envelope. Eight challenges: BADS standards against typical module widths; a consistent floor plate that keeps the product mix; cross-level uniformity; modular communal spaces; reengineering the core; consistent balconies; wet areas connected to the same module corridor risers; and repositioning the Building One core so it is not exposed on higher levels.

Offshore manufacture, local sign-off

Offshore manufacturing brings its own approval pathways and supply chain risks. Unison is able to engage with every authority a project requires: council building inspectors, product certifiers (BRANZ, CodeMark, WELS), fire authorities, licensing bodies, trade sign-offs, registered structural engineers and quarantine fumigation.

On supply, Unison can manage steel structural equivalence sign-off, independent testing of new products, incoming material inspections, and checking that supplier-issued compliance certificates are valid. Its quality assurance team adheres to ISO 9001:2015 with factory audits and in-process inspections.
